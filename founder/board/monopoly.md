# Monopoly lens
Score: 3/10 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong
- **Real product.** It works on the founder's machine and covers the hard parts: JavaScript-rendered discovery, lazy-load handling, settled animations, app-shell expansion and logged-in capture through "Copy as cURL" with no password shared. Each one is know-how that a weekend copycat would get wrong.
- **One possible small market to own.** That is screenshots of whole sites, behind a login, on four devices, in one run. Pages behind a login are the one feature that sounds clearly different.
- **History could build a moat.** The "time machine" angle (every change of every page, kept) is the only part that gets harder to leave over time: the longer a customer stays, the more history they would lose by leaving. It is not built yet.
- **Honest crawling (robots.txt, no bot-check bypass).** This could build a brand of trust with agencies working on client sites.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No distribution.** No channel has been tested, there is no marketing budget, there is no public domain and there are no customers. Under this lens, a product with no way to reach buyers is not a business. At $9 a month, any paid channel has to bring customers in very cheaply, and nobody knows what a customer costs to acquire. The CFO step must model that before any channel is chosen.
2. **A crowded market with nothing ten times better.** Screenshot, visual-regression and website-monitoring tools already reach this buyer. The brief does not name one competitor, and an unknown market is worse than a known crowded one. The features listed are a better bundle, not a breakthrough. Without one feature that is ten times better on something buyers care about, this is a fight on price, and the "Starter $4–5" and India pricing ideas show that fight has already started. Competitor research must map who serves freelancers and small agencies, at what price, and with which of these features.
3. **No durability or secret.** Headless Chrome is a commodity. The capture polish can be copied within months. There is no network effect, and economies of scale work against a solo founder, because storage costs grow with every customer. The $99 lifetime plan turns a recurring storage cost into a single payment. I see no secret the founder knows that others do not.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which narrow group can you own first: QA testers on apps behind a login, agencies doing redesign handovers, or compliance archiving? Why that one?
- How do your first 100 customers arrive, and what does each one cost? (CFO step)
- Name the five tools your buyer would try instead. What does each charge, and where are you ten times better? (competitor research)
- What do you know about this buyer that the existing tools have missed?
- Would buyers pay for history (the time machine) on its own? Does it change who stays after the first redesign project? (consumer panel)
- What does a heavy Agency user cost you in storage each month, and does the lifetime plan lose money? (CFO step)
- In month six, what stops a competitor from copying the login-capture feature?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
Not applicable; the vote is PASS. These would reopen it:
- Competitor research shows that one narrow group (for example, capture of logged-in apps for QA) has no tool that serves it well.
- One channel, tested, brings in paying customers at a cost the CFO step shows is recoverable at these prices.
- The time machine is built, and the consumer panel shows that it gives buyers a reason to stay subscribed after the first project.
- The lifetime plan is capped or removed until storage costs per user are known.
